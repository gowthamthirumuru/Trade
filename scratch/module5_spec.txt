L18: 1. Executive Summary
L19: Project APEX is a complete, zero-budget quantitative trading system that you will build and operate alone. Its purpose is not to “find a winning strategy” — it is to build a factory that continuously discovers, validates, filters, sizes, monitors, and retires strategies, the same loop every professional quant firm runs, rebuilt from free open-source components.
L20: The system rests on five pillars:
L21: A data lake of free, institutional-quality crypto market data (Binance public archive: tick-to-daily history since 2017, unlimited downloads, no account required), stored locally in Parquet + DuckDB.
L22: A strategy miner that automatically discovers candidate strategies — first by brute-force combinatorial search over indicator/filter/exit building blocks, later by genetic programming that evolves strategy logic itself, and finally by local machine learning (no paid AI APIs anywhere).
L23: A trade database (“the edge mine”) in which every trade from every backtest and every live session is stored with ~30 labels (strategy, parameters, pair, session, hour, day, volatility regime, trend regime, exit reason, MFE/MAE…). This database is the system’s most valuable permanent asset: it lets you mine when and where each strategy works — your original edge idea, implemented with statistical rigor.
L24: A validation lab that kills weak strategies before they cost money: walk-forward analysis, four kinds of Monte Carlo simulation, Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO), Deflated Sharpe Ratio, and regime stress tests. Expect to reject 95% of what the miner finds. That rejection rate is the system working correctly.
L25: A risk-first portfolio engine and command center: volatility-targeted position sizing, HRP/CVaR allocation across strategies, hard circuit breakers, and a Streamlit dashboard that hands you a clear “edge card” each session so manual execution becomes checklist execution.
L26: What success looks like (12–18 months): a system holding 100,000+ labeled trades; 5–10 validated strategies with out-of-sample-confirmed session/day/regime filters; a portfolio-level risk engine with circuit breakers you actually obey; a live track record of 100+ real trades whose expectancy matches the backtest within tolerance; and a monthly re-mining loop that keeps the edge fresh as markets evolve.
L27: Build time to first validated paper trade: ~12 weeks. To first live trade: ~5–6 months. Budget: ₹0 / $0.

L37: 3. Honest Constraints & Realistic Expectations
L38: This chapter exists so that month-6-you doesn’t quit out of misplaced expectations. Read it monthly.
L39: 3.1 You cannot beat institutions at their game. Funds have colocated servers, tick-level order flow, teams of PhDs, and billion-dollar data budgets. You will not out-speed or out-data them.
L40: 3.2 You can win the game they refuse to play. Institutional capital cannot bother with edges worth 0.3–1% per week on $5,000 — it’s meaningless to them and often too small or capacity-limited to trade. Retail-sized, patient, well-filtered edges in liquid-but-slow timeframes are exactly the niche this system targets.
L41: 3.3 Filtering turns break-even into profitable — it does not resurrect garbage. Day/hour/session/regime filtering (your core idea) reliably improves mediocre strategies; it rarely saves a strategy with no underlying edge. The miner’s job is to find raw material; the filter’s job is to refine it.
L42: 3.4 Most discovered “edges” are noise. If you test 100,000 variants, thousands look brilliant by pure chance. This is the multiple-comparisons problem, and it is why Part C’s Validation Lab (PBO, Deflated Sharpe, walk-forward) is non-negotiable. The uncomfortable truth: the better the backtest looks, the more skeptical you must be.
L43: 3.5 Edges decay. Market regimes shift; other participants arbitrage patterns away. Assume every edge has a half-life of 6–24 months. The monthly re-mining and edge-decay detector exist for this reason.
L44: 3.6 The operator is the biggest risk. Manual execution, revenge trading after losses, overriding the system “just this once,” stopping during drawdowns — operator behavior kills more retail systems than bad math. Chapter 29 is as important as any code.
L45: 3.7 Expectancy arithmetic (memorize):
L46: Expectancy = (win_rate × avg_win) − (loss_rate × avg_loss) − costs
L47: A strategy is tradeable only if Expectancy > 0 AFTER realistic
L48: fees + slippage + spread, AND n ≥ 100 OOS trades, AND it survives
L49: the Validation Lab.
L50: 3.8 Timeline honesty. Month 1–3: building, zero trading income. Month 4–6: paper trading, zero income. Month 6–12: small live trading, modest income if everything works. Compounding is slow at first and then not. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

L59: 5. The Twelve Core Design Principles
L60: These principles govern every build decision. When in doubt, the principle that says “be more conservative” wins.
L61: The database is the product. Strategies are disposable; the labeled trade database compounds in value forever. Never run a backtest whose trades aren’t logged.
L62: Costs first, always. Every backtest models fees, spread, and slippage from day one. A backtest without costs is fiction.
L63: In-sample is for searching, out-of-sample is for deciding. The miner never sees the test period. No exceptions, ever.
L64: Sample size or it didn’t happen. No slice, filter, or strategy is trusted below n=100 trades (50 = provisional only).
L65: Simple beats clever. A robust simple strategy beats a fragile complex one. Complexity must earn its place with OOS performance.
L66: Plateaus, not peaks. Choose parameter regions where neighbors also work; a lone peak is an overfit artifact.
L67: Kill ruthlessly. Rejecting 90–95% of candidates is the sign of a healthy pipeline, not a failure.
L68: Everything is reproducible. Every run is config-driven, seeded, logged, and re-runnable byte-for-byte.
L69: Local-first, zero-cost. If a component needs a paid API, it doesn’t belong in the core system.
L70: The system advises; discipline executes. Until auto-execution is earned, the human executes the checklist — not impulses.
L71: Risk is a budget, not an afterthought. Position size, exposure, and circuit breakers are computed by the Risk Engine, never by mood.
L72: Document as you build. A module without a completed QA checklist and a doc page is not “done.”

L224: open_time  TIMESTAMP UTC (bar open, Binance convention, ms→ts)
L225: open       DOUBLE

L227: low        DOUBLE
L228: close      DOUBLE

L281: 10.4 Implementation rules
L282: Point-in-time correctness: feature at bar t may only use data ≤ t (no lookahead). Every custom feature gets a lookahead unit test: shift the series by one bar, confirm feature values shift identically.
L283: Compute once, store, reuse: features are materialized to data/features/<pair>/<tf>.features.parquet, rebuilt nightly and versioned (feature_version column) when definitions change.
L284: No NaN leakage: warm-up rows (first ~252 bars) are marked, not silently dropped mid-pipeline; strategies decide whether to trade warm-up regions.
L285: Deterministic: same input → same output; no randomness anywhere in this module.

L310: 11.3 Miner-1: Brute-force combinatorial miner (build FIRST)
L311: Building blocks (each a versioned function returning a boolean series):
L312: Search strategy — staged, not full-cartesian (full cartesian of the above is ~10⁸; too much):
L313: Stage 1 — solo scan: every entry trigger alone × timeframes × pairs (~30 × 4 × 20 = 2,400 runs). Establish baseline expectancy of each trigger.
L314: Stage 2 — pair-up: top 30% triggers × every filter × fixed exit (~10 × 20 × 12 × 4 × 20 ≈ 192,000 runs — vectorbt handles this overnight).
L315: Stage 3 — exit search: top 500 combos × all exit models × exit params.
L316: Stage 4 — plateau analysis: for each survivor, perturb each parameter ±20%; score = fraction of neighbors still profitable (plateau score ≥ 0.6 required).
L317: Ranking score (in-sample, pre-validation):
L318: score = expectancy_bps × log10(n_trades) × plateau_score × consistency
L319: where consistency = fraction of profitable years in-sample
L320: 11.4 Miner-2: Genetic programming miner (Phase 9)
L321: Engine: DEAP (genetic algorithm framework) + gplearn-style expression trees.
L322: Genome: expression tree over feature primitives, e.g. AND(GT(RSI(14), 68), LT(close, MUL(VWAP, 0.98))).
L323: Fitness: OOS-segment-1 Sharpe × sqrt(n) − λ×tree_depth — penalize complexity explicitly (λ in config).
L324: Anti-overfit mechanics: (a) fitness evaluated on in-sample sub-segment A, selection pressure from sub-segment B (GeneTrader-style “selection bar”: the distribution of best-of-N luck); (b) max tree depth 6; (c) island model (separate populations, occasional migration) for diversity; (d) final candidates must pass the full Validation Lab like everything else — genetic search is a hypothesis generator, not a shortcut around validation.
L325: Compute budget: overnight runs (~8h); population 500 × 40 generations.
L326: 11.5 Miner-3: Local ML layer (Phase 10+, free)
L327: Task framing: predict P(trade profitable | features at entry) using the Trade Database itself as labeled training data — the system literally learns from its own edge mine.
L328: Models: gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) + logistic regression baseline. SHAP for interpretability (which features drive success — often reveals unknown session/regime structure).
L329: Validation: purged, embargoed time-series CV (López de Prado method) to prevent leakage; predictions only ever trade-sized through the Risk Engine, never naked.
L330: Hard rule: ML output is a filter/size adjuster on top of validated strategies, never a standalone black-box strategy, until you have 12+ months of live evidence.
L331: 11.6 Mining governance (what makes this professional)
L332: Every mining run is registered in runs/ with: config hash, git commit, date range, variant count, machine, duration.
L333: Trial budget accounting: the miner logs n_variants_tested — this number feeds the Deflated Sharpe calculation. You cannot compute honest significance without knowing how many things you tried.
L334: Survivors are candidates, never strategies. Only the Validation Lab can promote a candidate.
L335: Miner never sees post-2022 data. Enforced at the data-access layer.

L1115: 22. Formula & Metrics Library
L1116: Expectancy (R): E[R] = (WR × avg_win_R) − ((1−WR) × avg_loss_R) — must be > 0 net of costs.
L1117: Profit factor: PF = gross_profit / |gross_loss|; ≥ 1.3 to survive screening, ≥ 1.15 OOS.
L1118: Sharpe (annualized): SR = mean(r_daily) / std(r_daily) × √365 (crypto trades 365 days).
L1119: Sortino: downside deviation only; preferred for skewed crypto returns.
L1120: Calmar: CAGR / |maxDD|; ≥ 1.0 target at portfolio level.
L1121: Max drawdown: max_t (peak_t − equity_t) / peak_t on the equity curve.
L1122: Walk-Forward Efficiency: WFE = ann_return_OOS / ann_return_IS; ≥ 0.5.
L1123: Deflated Sharpe Ratio: DSR = Φ( (SR̂ − E[max SR under H0]) / SE(SR̂) ) where E[max SR] grows with √(2 ln N) for N variants tried, adjusted for skew/kurtosis — full formula implemented from Bailey & López de Prado (2014).
L1124: PBO: fraction of CSCV combinations where IS-optimal variant ranks below OOS median; logit transformation per López de Prado (2018).
L1125: Historical VaR_α: quantile(r_daily, 1−α); CVaR_α: mean of returns beyond VaR.
L1126: Parametric VaR (GARCH): VaR_95 = μ̂ + σ̂_t+1 × z_0.05, σ̂ from GARCH(1,1) via arch.
L1127: Position size: qty = equity × risk% / stop_distance% (× portfolio weight × decay multiplier).
L1128: Kelly fraction: f* = WR − (1−WR)/payoff; deploy at most f*/4.
L1129: Kupiec VaR backtest: LR = −2 ln[(1−p)^(T−N) p^N] + 2 ln[(1−N/T)^(T−N) (N/T)^N] ~ χ²(1).
L1130: Multiple-testing corrections: Bonferroni α/m; Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at q=0.05 (default for scans).

L1291: Appendix A — Glossary
L1292: Alpha — return attributable to skill/edge rather than market exposure. Backtest — simulation of a strategy on historical data. Circuit breaker — pre-committed automatic risk limit. L1351: G.1 Expectancy arithmetic
L1352: Strategy: WR 44%, avg win +1.9R, avg loss −1.0R, round-trip cost 0.06R.
L1353: E[R] = 0.44 × 1.9 − 0.56 × 1.0 − 0.06 = 0.836 − 0.56 − 0.06 = +0.216R/trade
L1354: At 0.75% risk/trade and 60 trades/month: E[monthly] ≈ 60 × 0.216 × 0.75% ≈ +9.7%/month
L1355: At 0.75% risk/trade and 8 trades/month:  E[monthly] ≈ +1.3%/month
L1356: Lesson: expectancy × frequency × size = income. Filters trade frequency for expectancy — the edge analysis exists to optimize that product, not any single term.
L1357: G.2 Position sizing (three examples)
L1358: Equity $4,000; risk 0.75% → $30 at risk. - Ex 1: ETHUSDT, entry 3,200, stop 3,168 (1% below). qty = 30 / 0.01 = $3,000 notional ≈ 0.937 ETH. L1359: G.3 Kelly criterion sanity check
L1360: WR 45%, payoff 1.8. f* = 0.45 − 0.55/1.8 = 0.144 → quarter-Kelly = 3.6% of equity per trade — far above our 0.75% fixed-fractional. Conclusion: fixed-fractional is the conservative governor; Kelly is only ever a ceiling cross-check, never the sizing engine at our sample sizes.
L1361: G.4 Monte Carlo interpretation
L1362: 10,000 reshuffles of a 600-trade sequence with maxDD realized 11%:
L1363: MC maxDD percentiles:  50th 12% | 75th 15% | 90th 18% | 95th 21% | 99th 27%

L1365: G.5 VaR & CVaR by hand
L1366: Daily portfolio returns over 500 days, sorted worst 25 (= 5%): −3.2% … −1.9%. - Historical VaR95 ≈ 1.9% (the 25th-worst day boundary) - CVaR95 ≈ mean of worst 25 ≈ −2.5% - GARCH parametric: σ̂(t+1) = 1.15% → VaR95 = 1.645 × 1.15% ≈ 1.89% (consistent ✓) - Kupiec check: expected 25 exceptions, observed 28 → within acceptance region (don’t reject model).
L1367: G.6 Deflated Sharpe intuition
L1368: Same strategy, same Sharpe 1.4, two contexts: - Tried 10 variants → E[max SR | H0] ≈ 0.8 → DSR comfortably positive → PASS - Tried 100,000 variants → E[max SR | H0] ≈ 1.5 → DSR ≈ 0 → FAIL Identical performance, opposite verdicts — because luck’s expected maximum grows with trials. This is why the miner’s trial accounting (n_variants) is sacred.
L1369: G.7 Risk-of-ruin framing
L1370: With E[R] = +0.2, σ(R) = 1.2, risk 0.75%/trade: risk of hitting the −20% stop before doubling is small (single digits %). At 3% risk/trade it multiplies several-fold. The 0.5–1% band isn’t timidity; it’s what keeps the −20% event rare enough that PB-4 is a procedure you’ll likely never run.

L1490: PART H — TRADER’S REFERENCE LIBRARY
L1491: 33. Statistics Primer for Traders
L1492: The minimum statistics you must understand to operate this system honestly. Each concept is stated, then translated into trading terms.
L1493: 33.1 Distributions. Trade P&L is a random draw from the strategy’s return distribution. Crypto trade returns are fat-tailed (extreme moves more common than a normal distribution predicts) and often skewed (trend systems: many small losses, few large wins — negative skew of losses, positive skew of P&L tail). Consequence: never use normal-distribution assumptions for risk; use historical/bootstrapped distributions (which is exactly why our Monte Carlo reshuffles real trades instead of simulating Gaussians).
L1494: 33.2 Sample size and standard error. The standard error of a mean shrinks as 1/√n. A slice of 25 trades with expectancy +0.3R and σ(R)=1.2 has SE ≈ 0.24 — the “edge” is 1.25 standard errors from zero, i.e., indistinguishable from luck. At n=100 the same numbers give SE ≈ 0.12 (2.5σ — worth attention); at n=400, 5σ (real). This single formula — z = E / (σ/√n) — is the mathematical spine of the n≥100 rule, the decay detector, and the live-vs-backtest drift test.
L1495: 33.3 Hypothesis testing. Null hypothesis H0: “this slice has no edge (true mean = 0).” The p-value is P(data this extreme | H0 true) — NOT the probability the edge is real. p < 0.05 means: if there were no edge, data like this would appear only 5% of the time. With one test, that’s persuasive. With 500 tests (one hour×day scan), you EXPECT ~25 false positives at p<0.05 — which is why §33.5 exists.
L1496: 33.4 Confidence intervals. “Expectancy +0.21R, 95% CI [+0.07, +0.35]” beats “expectancy +0.21R” every time. The CI tells you the plausible range; if it crosses zero, the estimate is noise-grade regardless of how good the point looks. Bootstrap CIs (resample the trades 5,000×, take percentiles) handle the fat tails correctly and are built into the slice engine.
L1497: 33.5 Multiple comparisons and FDR. Every additional hypothesis tested inflates the false-positive rate. Corrections: Bonferroni (divide α by number of tests — simple, conservative) and Benjamini–Hochberg (controls the expected fraction of false discoveries — more powerful, our default). The edge engine never reports a raw p-value without its correction context. Rule of thumb: if you scanned 168 hour×day cells, treat any unadjusted p < 0.05 as meaningless; only BH-passing slices may become cards.
L1498: 33.6 Regression to the mean. Extreme observed performance (the best variant of 100,000) is almost always part skill, part luck — and the luck part evaporates forward. Expect OOS performance to be worse than IS, systematically (that’s why WFE ≥ 0.5 passes but WFE = 1.0 should make you suspicious of a leak).
L1499: 33.7 Stationarity. Statistics assumes the distribution stays stable. Markets are non-stationary — vol clusters, regimes shift, edges die. This is THE fundamental problem of quantitative trading, and the system’s entire monitoring layer (decay detection, monthly re-validation, card expiry) is the engineered response to it.
L1500: 33.8 Independence and autocorrelation. Trades cluster in time; a losing streak is not 10 independent losses but often one bad regime. Block bootstrap (MC #3) exists because reshuffle assumes independence; when the two disagree, trust the block version.
L1501: 33.9 Bayesian intuition (no math required). Prior: most strategy ideas fail (>90%). Evidence: a good backtest. Posterior: still probably fails — the validation gauntlet is how you accumulate enough evidence to update a skeptical prior. Operators who internalize this stop falling in love with backtests.
L1502: 33.10 The two errors. Type I: trading a strategy that doesn’t work (costs money). Type II: discarding one that does (costs opportunity). Retail optimizes against Type II (afraid to miss anything); professionals optimize against Type I (afraid to trade noise). This system is deliberately Type-I-averse: capital preservation beats missed chances, always.
L1503: 34. Crypto Market Microstructure Primer
L1504: What you must know about the terrain you’re trading.
L1505: 34.1 Perpetuals vs spot. Perps track spot via the funding mechanism: when perp trades above spot, longs pay shorts (positive funding) and vice versa. Funding is a direct sentiment gauge (extreme = crowded positioning → S5 fade strategy) and a real cash flow if you hold perp positions. Spot has no funding but also no short option on most retail venues — most short strategies in this system assume perp execution.
L1506: 34.2 Liquidity cycles. Despite 24/7 trading, volume follows a daily wave: deepest during the Europe–US overlap (roughly 12:00–16:00 UTC), thinnest in late-Asia hours and weekends. Consequences: (a) slippage varies 2–3× across the day — the cost model’s session-varying slippage is not optional; (b) breakout strategies work best when participation arrives (session opens); (c) weekend price action is lower-quality — always test weekend exclusion as a filter.
L1507: 34.3 Volatility regimes. Crypto vol clusters hard: quiet weeks of 1% daily ranges punctuated by 10% days. ATR-percentile regime labels capture this. Mean-reversion strategies harvest the quiet; momentum strategies harvest the storms; a strategy blind to vol regime is a coin flip on which world it’s in.
L1508: 34.4 Event structure. Macro events (FOMC, US CPI, payrolls) hit crypto sharply — the 30 minutes after release are spread-widening, wick-heavy chaos. Trade them only via a dedicated event strategy (S6) or stand aside (F08 blackout). Token-specific events (unlocks, mainnet launches, exchange listings) matter for alts.
L1509: 34.5 Cross-pair correlation. Alts are highly correlated to BTC (typical 30d correlation 0.6–0.9). Five alt longs ≈ one big BTC beta position — the correlation guard and max same-direction limits exist for exactly this. It also means multi-pair “diversification” is partly illusory; true diversification comes from strategy family diversity (trend vs MR vs carry).
L1510: 34.6 Exchange microstructure. Binance spot taker fees ~10bps (less with BNB discount/volume tiers — recalibrate config to YOUR actual tier), perp taker ~4–5bps. Market orders cross the spread; limit orders earn it but risk non-fill. In backtests, always pay taker costs unless the strategy is explicitly limit-based with a fill model — optimism here is the classic way backtests lie.
L1511: 34.7 Weekend and holiday effects. Institutional participation drops on weekends and US holidays; ranges compress but stop-hunts in thin books produce vicious wicks. Test, don’t assume: some pairs show real weekend edges; most show noise with worse fills.
L1512: 34.8 Stablecoin basis and risk. P&L in USDT is P&L in a dollar-IOU. Operationally: keep balances modest, sweep profits, and remember depeg tail-risk exists. The system’s custody rule (monthly sweeps) is non-negotiable.

L1520: 38. Self-Interrogation FAQ
L1521: The questions you’ll secretly ask yourself at 2 AM, answered in advance.
L1522: “The backtest is incredible — why shouldn’t I just trade it now?” Because an unvalidated backtest is a hypothesis, not a result. Run the gauntlet. If it’s real, it will still be real in two weeks.
L1523: “The miner found nothing this month — is it broken?” Check the controls (A7.3/A7.4). If controls pass, nothing-found is a legitimate market state. Mining dry spells are normal; forcing output is how noise gets promoted.
L1524: “A strategy passed everything but feels wrong.” Write down why. If the reason is mechanical (“I can’t explain the logic”), it doesn’t trade — the 60-second rule. If it’s vibes, trade it small; feelings aren’t evidence either.
L1525: “Three losing weeks — is the edge dead?” Compute the z-score (the dashboard does). A −1.5% week happens at 0.75% risk with no edge problems at all. The detector tells you when it’s statistically meaningful; until then, variance is the boring answer.
L1526: “Can I just add one discretionary trade?” That’s violation #1 toward lockout. The system exists because discretionary-you underperforms systematic-you. Journal the urge instead — urges logged weekly are useful psychology data.
L1527: “Why not just buy a commercial tool (StrategyQuant etc.)?” Budget is zero, and black-box mining without the validation literacy this build gives you would lose money anyway. You’re building the tool AND the operator.
L1528: “When do I scale up?” §30.1’s sizing-up law. Never earlier, never because of a hot streak, never because of impatience.
L1529: “What if everything fails after a year?” Then you’ll have something worth more than a strategy: a verified negative result set, institutional-grade process skills, and a database of 100k+ analyzed trades — assets that transfer to any future trading, quant job applications, or fund-raising credibility. The only true failure mode is skipping the process.

L1550: 41. Case Studies — Anatomy of a Failure & of a Success
L1551: Two composite case studies showing the system thinking end-to-end. Expect your own versions of both within the first year.
L1552: 41.1 Anatomy of a failure: “The Perfect Tuesday”
L1553: Discovery. A Stage-2 mining run surfaces T05+F05(Tue)+X02(2R) on BNBUSDT: expectancy +0.61R, n=88, PF 2.1. The Tuesday-only filter looks like magic.
L1554: Red flags the system raises automatically: 1. n=88 < 100 → provisional, can’t be carded. Operator temptation: “close enough.” It is not. 2. The scanner tested 168 day×hour cells × 500+ combos — BH correction requires p < 0.0012; this slice sits at p = 0.008. Fails FDR. On noise alone we’d expect several cells this good. 3. Stability check: 100% of the edge comes from the 2021 bull half; the 2022 half is flat. Single-regime dependence. 4. Plateau: moving the day filter to Mon or Wed → negative. The “edge” is one cell wide — a spike, not a plateau.
L1555: Verdict: KILLED at Gate 1-adjacent checks; archived with reason MULTIPLE_TESTING + REGIME_FRAGILE. Cost of this discipline: nothing. Cost without it: ~3 months and a real-money drawdown discovering the same thing.
L1556: Lesson the database keeps: six months later, monthly re-mine finds the same Tuesday pattern again — now it’s in the kill archive, cross-referenced, and auto-flagged. The system remembers so you don’t re-pay tuition.
L1557: 41.2 Anatomy of a success: “Boring Session Breakout”
L1558: Discovery. T09 session-breakout variant on ETHUSDT (the §C2.4 walkthrough): +0.19R, n=631 — unimpressive next to the Tuesday miracle.
L1559: Why it survives: n large; plateau 0.875 (neighbors work); profitable in both halves; session-slice p_adj = 0.003 after BH; OOS +0.21R (expectancy went UP out-of-sample — happens; treat with same skepticism, verified no leak); WFE 0.71; MC dd_p95 13%; PBO 0.11; DSR p=0.02.
L1560: Life after validation: paper 41 trades +0.16R → live micro 60 trades +0.18R → decay z never below −1.2 → promoted to CORE at 100 live trades, sizing multiplier 1.25. Contributes a boring +1.2%/month at 0.75% risk.
L1561: The meta-lesson: the failure LOOKED better in every screenshot. The success looked boring in all of them. The system’s gates exist because humans cannot tell these apart by looking — the failed one had the higher backtest expectancy by 3×. This is the entire point of the architecture.

L1688: Appendix N — Expanded Glossary of Quant Terms
L1689: Alpha decay — the tendency of a discovered edge to weaken as markets adapt. Anchored walk-forward — walk-forward where the training window’s start stays fixed while it grows. Autocorrelation — correlation of a series with its own past; streaks in trade P&L. Basis — price difference between perp and spot. Beta — sensitivity to the market factor (BTC for alts). Block bootstrap — resampling contiguous chunks to preserve autocorrelation. Breadth — number of independent bets; Grinold’s law: skill × √breadth. Calmar ratio — CAGR ÷ max drawdown. Chandelier exit — ATR-based trailing stop hanging from the highest high. Cointegration — long-run statistical linkage between non-stationary series (pairs trading basis). Cross-sectional — comparing many assets at one time (vs time-series: one asset across time). CSCV — combinatorially symmetric cross-validation; the PBO machinery. CVaR / Expected Shortfall — average loss in the worst α% of cases. Deflated Sharpe — Sharpe significance deflated by trial count and non-normality. Drawdown duration — time spent below the equity peak; hurts more than depth. Edge — positive expectancy that persists out-of-sample and net of costs. Embargo — gap enforced between train and test windows to prevent leakage. Expectancy — mean P&L per trade (R or bps). Exposure — fraction of time/capital in the market. Fat tails — extreme events more frequent than Gaussian prediction. Fill model — assumptions about how orders execute in backtest. Funding rate — perp mechanism payment anchoring perp to spot. GARCH — volatility clustering model; forecasts tomorrow’s vol. HRP — clustering-based allocation needing no covariance inversion. Information Coefficient — rank correlation of signal vs forward return. Intrabar ambiguity — unknowable order of high/low within one bar. Kelly criterion — growth-optimal bet fraction; used fractionally if at all. Kupiec test — statistical check of VaR model exception rate. Leakage — any future information contaminating the past; the original sin. MAE/MFE — worst/best excursion during a trade’s life. Multiple comparisons problem — more tests ⇒ more false discoveries. Overfitting — fitting noise; performs in-sample, dies live. PBO — probability the selected config is overfit. Plateau — parameter region where neighbors also perform. Point-in-time — data as known at that moment, no revisions. Profit factor — gross profit ÷ gross loss. Purged CV — cross-validation removing overlapping label windows. R-multiple — P&L in units of initial risk. Regime — persistent market state with stable statistics. Risk parity — allocating so each component contributes equal risk. Sharpe ratio — excess return per unit volatility. Slippage — execution price worse than decision price. Sortino — Sharpe using downside deviation only. Stationarity — stable statistical properties over time. Stochastic dominance — one distribution systematically better than another. Survivorship bias — measuring only what survived. Tail risk — exposure to extreme, rare losses. Time-stop — exit after fixed holding period. Turnover — trading frequency; drives cost drag. Type I/II error — trading noise / discarding signal. VaR — loss level not exceeded with probability α. Volatility targeting — scaling size to hold portfolio vol constant. Walk-forward efficiency — OOS ÷ IS annualized performance. Wick — bar extreme beyond body; rejection evidence. z-score — distance from mean in standard errors.
